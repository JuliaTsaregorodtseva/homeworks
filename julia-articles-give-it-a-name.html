Why a name, a description, a term, and not the? One short explanation, then four quick exercises. About 15 minutes.

The two sentences

— Brainrot has always existed. We just finally gave it a name.

There is a term in psychology for this. We just finally gave it a description.

Why “a” and not “the”?

  1. The noun is countable and singular. A name, a term, a description (= one account of something) are single, countable things, so they can't stand alone: we need a or the.
  2. It's new information. The listener doesn't know which name or which description yet. Until now the thing didn't have one. The sentence tells us that it now has one.
  3. Which one doesn't matter here. The point of the sentence is the fact of naming, not the name itself. We gave it a name ≈ “it's no longer nameless”. As soon as you say which name, it becomes the: the name “brainrot”, the term “spotlight effect”.
And the dash? Brainrot has always existed: an abstract idea in general (like love, boredom, nostalgia) takes no article, and here it's also used like a name.
agive it a … a family of phrases with the same logic
  • Naming and labelling. give it a name · give it a label · put a name to it · there's a word for that · coin a term
  • Describing. give a description of… · give it a definition · give it a voice
  • Trying. give it a try / a go / a shot · give it a second chance · give it a second look
  • But uncountable nouns: no a. give it time · give it some thought · give it attention · give it priority
