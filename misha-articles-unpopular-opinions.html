Three choices, one question

For every noun you say, English makes you decide how much the other person already knows. That's the whole job of an article: it signals shared knowledge. It isn't decoration. About 25 minutes, no writing.

The article finder

Think of any noun in a sentence you want to say and answer the questions.

1a / an one of many, and you can't tell which
  • Introducing something new. I heard a take yesterday that made me close the app.
  • Saying what something is. That's a pretty unpopular opinion. · She's a journalist. · It was an argument, not a conversation.
  • Rates and amounts. twice a week · ten pounds a ticket
  • After what and such. What an awful take! · such a strange thing to believe
  • Choose by sound, never by spelling.a university · a one-sided debate · a European habit · an hour · an honest opinion · an MP
2the you and I both know which one
  • Second mention. I read a comment. The comment was three words long.
  • Only one in this situation. Could you close the window? · What did the teacher say?
  • Only one in the world we share. the internet · the media · the government · the environment
  • The words after the noun say which one. the view that nobody defends · the opinions of my parentsThis is the one good students forget. If you ask “which one?” and the sentence already answers, you need the.
  • Superlative, only, same, first, next, last. the most unpopular opinion in the room · the only person who agreed
  • the + adjective = a whole group of people. the young · the rich · the silent majority
3no article things in general, not particular ones
  • Plural, in general. Opinions are cheap. · People say they want honesty.
  • Uncountable and abstract, in general. Honesty is rare online. · Outrage travels faster than information.
  • Most names. Misha · Georgia · Tbilisi · English
  • Set phrases. at home · at work · by bus · go to bed / school / universityNo article when you mean the purpose of the place: I went to school (to study) vs I went to the school (the building).

Eight traps that catch good students

You know the three rules. Almost every upper-intermediate mistake happens in the small print below.

Big abstract nouns in general

The society expects you to agree. → Society expects you to agree.

also life, nature, history, technology, love
…but these always take the

the media · the internet · the environment · the economy · the government

but social media, television (watch TV), nature
most people, not the most people

Most people never change their minds. · Most of the people I follow are like that.

most of people and the most people are always wrong
Uncountables never take a / an

an advice → advice / some advice / a piece of advice

information, news, research, evidence, feedback, progress, knowledge
Abstract noun + of / that / to

the importance of listening · the idea that everyone deserves a platform

the phrase after the noun pins the idea down
One determiner is enough

the my opinion → my opinion · a some views → some views

my, this, some, every, no already do the article's job
the number of / a number of

The number of complaints is rising. · A number of people have complained.

the number = the quantity (singular verb) · a number = several (plural verb)
Fixed phrases: learn them whole

on the one hand · in the minority · a matter of opinion · to a certain extent · make a point · in my opinion

How to actually learn them

Six techniques that work

  1. Ask “which one?” every time. Before every noun, ask yourself “which one?”. If the context or the words after the noun answer it, use the. If they don't, it's a (one thing) or nothing (general).
  2. Learn nouns with their article, as chunks. Don't learn internet, learn the internet. Don't learn opinion, learn a matter of opinion and in my opinion. Keep a list of chunks and add to it.
  3. Check [C] or [U] in the dictionary. Most a / an mistakes are really uncountable mistakes. If the dictionary says [U] (advice, research), you can't use a / an.
  4. Read with three highlighters. Take any short article and mark every a / an, the and noun with no article in three colours, then explain each one to yourself. The Read tab does this for you, so try it there first.
  5. Do one editing pass just for nouns. After you write something, go back and read it only for nouns, asking the three questions. Don't check articles while you're writing; check them afterwards.
  6. Collect your own mistakes. Write down every article your teacher corrects, with the reason. You'll see you make the same 3–4 mistakes again and again, and those are the ones worth drilling.
