Rule 1 · Grammar focus

look · sound · taste · smell · feel

We use these verbs to describe the impression something gives us through our senses. They are followed by three different patterns — the choice depends on what comes next.

sense verb + adjective
This soup tastes horrible.
You look exhausted.
sense verb + like + noun
This meat tastes like beef.
She looks like her mother.
sense verb + as if / as though + clause
It looks as if someone has been here.
You sound as though you've got a cold.

How to choose

Next comes…UseExample
an adjectivenothing in betweenIt smells delicious.
a noun (phrase)likeIt smells like fresh bread.
a clause (subject + verb)as if / as thoughIt smells as if something is burning.
seem works the same way: He seems tired. · It seems as if nobody cares. You can also say He seems to be tired.
like + clause (It looks like it has been repaired) is very common in conversation, but in writing and exams it's better to use as if / as though.

Careful!

He seems tiredly.
He seems tired.
The music sounds beautifully.
The music sounds beautiful.
This tastes as beef.
This tastes like beef.
You look like tired.
You look tired.

Why an adjective, not an adverb? These verbs describe the subject (the soup is horrible), not how an action is done.

Rule 2 · Revision

The passive and passive reporting

Passive = be (in the right tense) + past participle. We use it when the action matters more than who does it.

TensePassive
present continuousA new hospital is being built.
past continuousThe bridge was being repaired when we drove past.
present perfectThe documents have been signed.
futureThe winners will be announced on Friday.

Passive reporting (formal, news style)

PatternExample
It is said / thought / believed that + clauseIt is said that the castle is haunted.
subject + is said to + infinitive (now)The castle is said to be haunted.
subject + is said to have + past participle (earlier)The thief is believed to have escaped through the window.
Choose the infinitive by time: same time as the reporting → to be / to do; earlier → to have done; in progress → to be doing.

have something done

have + object + past participle = someone else does it for you (usually you pay). In informal English, get is also used.

I had my hair cut yesterday.
We're having our flat painted next week.
I cut my hair at the hairdresser's.
I had my hair cut at the hairdresser's.
I had cut my hair.
I had my hair cut.
Rule 3 · Revision

Reporting verbs

Each reporting verb has its own pattern. Learn them as chunks.

PatternVerbsExample
verb + sb to doadvise, warn, tell, persuade, remindShe advised me to see a lawyer.
verb + to dorefuse, agree, promise, offer, threatenHe refused to give me a day off.
verb + -ingdeny, admit, suggest, recommendTom denied breaking the vase.
verb + preposition + -ingapologise for, insist on, accuse sb ofKate apologised for being late.
Negative: warn / advise sb not to do — He warned us not to swim there. · suggest never takes sb to do: She suggested me to go → She suggested going / that I (should) go.
