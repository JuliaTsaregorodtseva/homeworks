Step 1 · The rule

Opinion first. Facts follow in a fixed order.

When two or more adjectives come before a noun, English puts them in a fairly fixed order. What you think comes first; what anyone can see or check comes after, from general to specific — the closer to the noun, the more it says about what the thing really is.

a lovely big old round green spotted Chinese ceramic teapot — possible, but nobody talks like this!

Try it

Build the teapot

Switch groups on and off in any order you like. The adjectives always jump into the right place — and watch what happens to a / an.

How many?

Usually 2, sometimes 3 — not 8.

In real speech and writing, two adjectives before a noun is normal and three is the usual maximum. The full order is a map that tells you where each one goes, not a target to hit.

Examples
Flexible pairs. Native speakers sometimes swap age ↔ shape and colour ↔ pattern (you will see both an old round table and a round old table). These pairs are not tested in this homework — every other order is.
Step 2 · Building phrases

Opinion or fact?

An opinion adjective is something another person could disagree with: lovely, awful, expensive, comfortable, boring, smart, delicious. A fact adjective can be checked: big, old, round, red, French, wooden. Opinion always goes first.

Two opinions? General words that can describe almost anything (nice, lovely, great, awful) usually come before more specific ones (comfortable, friendly, tasty): a nice comfortable chair, a lovely friendly teacher.
Type / purpose

The "what kind?" word sits next to the noun

Words that tell us what kind of thing it is, or what it is for, go last — after material. They are often nouns themselves.

a or an?

Look at the first adjective, not the noun

The article depends on the sound of the word right after it.

It's the sound that counts: a useful tip (/juː/), an honest answer (silent h).

Style tip

Too many adjectives? Move some after the noun.

Instead of piling up five adjectives, keep two or three and put the rest into a with-phrase, a made of-phrase or a second sentence.

Step 3 · Commas and "and"

Nothing, a comma, or and?

SituationWhat to doExample
Different groups before a nounNothing — no comma, no anda lovely old house
Same group before a noun (two opinions)A comma or anda kind, generous man / a kind and generous man
Coloursand (with commas in a longer list)a black and white cat; a red, white and blue flag
After the verb (be, look, seem, feel…)and before the last oneThe flat was bright and quiet. It was cold, wet and windy.
After the verb, the strict order matters much less: The film was long and boring and The film was boring and long are both fine. The fixed order is a rule for adjectives before a noun.
